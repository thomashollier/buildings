# Blind fidelity review — Judge A

Scope: 16 flat elevation drawings (img00–img15) seen with no labels. Rendering simplifications are ignored: flat colours, 4-pane glazing, a green strip for "ground" even where it would be water. Only the architecture is judged.

## How widths were estimated
- **Amsterdam-type drawings:** scaled from storey height. I used 3.0–3.3 m per storey; real canal-house storeys are 3–4 m, so these widths may be about 10% low. I cross-checked against the door: 2.5–2.7 m including the transom, which is correct for a canal house.
- **Venetian drawings:** scaled from the water gate. I took its total height to the arch apex as about 4 m. A 3 m storey would make every Venetian palazzo implausibly small, because real *piani nobili* are 4.5–6 m high.
- **Byzantine arcades:** scaled from the ground arcade, taking the storey as about 6 m.
- **Confidence:** about ±20% on all widths.

## Amsterdam family — shared observations

**Right in every Amsterdam drawing:**
- Narrow, deep-red brick fronts.
- A raised ground floor (*bel-etage*) reached by a stone stoop, with a half-sunk basement (*souterrain*) whose windows sit below the stoop.
- A door with a transom light, placed in an end bay.
- 2 or 3 regular bays, 4 storeys.
- A gable with a loading door and a hoist-beam stub at the apex.

**Wrong in every Amsterdam drawing:**
- **(a) Stoop geometry.** The single flight climbs parallel to the façade and arrives *beside* the door. In front of the door the treads are still 0.5–1 m below the sill, and there is no landing (*bordes*). A real stoop either has a landing in front of the door, or runs perpendicular to the façade, or has two flights. It also has iron railings, which are missing here. See the Prinsengracht row (no. 15): every stoop has a railing and a landing on the door axis.
- **(b) Window heights barely diminish.** Floors 2–4 have identical windows. Real canal houses step them down storey by storey, most strongly after 1700.
- **(c) No sandstone.** There are no sandstone dressings, lintels, string courses or carved claw-pieces. The gables are plain brick silhouettes.
- **(d) Glazing.** Glazing bars are ignored, which I accept as rendering. Only the proportions are judged.

### img00 — Amsterdam neck gable (*halsgevel*), 3 bays
1. **Identification:** Amsterdam canal house with a neck gable, c.1640–1700 (neck gables are commonest 1640–1770). Frontage about 7.5–8.5 m. Confidence: high.
2. **Comparables:** Herengracht 362/364 (no. 14); Prinsengracht 287–305 (no. 15); Prinsengracht 307 (no. 16).
   - **Right:** the 3-bay base. The neck is one bay wide and carries a window with a loading door and hoist beam above it, under a triangular pediment. The shoulders spring at full façade width. The neck is 2 levels tall, which is typical.
   - **Wrong:** the claw-pieces (*klauwstukken*) are plain brick quarter-circles. They should be carved sandstone scrolls, and they are the signature element of a neck gable. There are no sandstone corner blocks under the shoulders. The stoop arrives beside the door with no landing. The bel-etage windows are only about 10% taller than the upper ones.
3. **Scores:** R 5, P 3. **Most damaging error:** the bare brick claw-pieces, with no sandstone ornament at the shoulders.

### img06 — Amsterdam bell gable (*klokgevel*), 2 bays
1. **Identification:** Amsterdam bell-gabled canal house, c.1660–1790. Frontage about 6–6.5 m. Confidence: high.
2. **Comparables:** Prinsengracht 305 (no. 16); Prinsengracht 287–305 row (no. 15); Brouwersgracht bell gables nearby (no. 17).
   - **Right:** the 2-bay base, 4 storeys, a bell outline with a rounded crown, and a hoist beam on the axis.
   - **Wrong:**
     - The loading door sits in the *right* bay, off axis, while the hoist beam is on the centreline. On a bell gable the loading door is always centred under the beam, because the beam has to be above the hoist opening.
     - The gable holds two openings side by side. That is normal for a wide spout gable, but on a bell gable one central door is normal.
     - The bell's shoulders are drawn as separate concave flares attached to a vertical shaft. A real bell gable is one continuous convex–concave outline, usually finished with sandstone ornament.
3. **Scores:** R 4, P 2. **Most damaging error:** the off-axis loading door under a centred hoist beam.

### img07 — Amsterdam cornice house (*lijstgevel*), 3 bays
1. **Identification:** Amsterdam cornice-fronted canal house, c.1720–1850. Frontage about 6–6.5 m. Confidence: medium. Without the stoop it could pass as a US brick row house.
2. **Comparables:** the cornice house in the Brouwersgracht 264 photo (no. 17); Herengracht cornice fronts in no. 14; Prinsengracht row (no. 15).
   - **Right:** a flat top with a projecting cornice on blocks, 3 bays × 4 storeys, a raised entrance and basement windows.
   - **Wrong:**
     - Nothing rises above the cornice. Most Amsterdam *lijstgevels* have a central dormer or attic (*dakkapel*) with a hoist beam, and the hoist beam is the Amsterdam signature (see the dormer in the Brouwersgracht photo).
     - The cornice is a plain thick slab. It is usually a moulded wooden cornice, often with a frieze and attic windows set in it.
     - The top-floor windows are noticeably squatter, which is good. The rest repeats.
3. **Scores:** R 3, P 3. **Most damaging error:** no hoist beam or dormer, which leaves the house generic rather than Amsterdam.

### img10 — Amsterdam step gable (*trapgevel*), 2 bays
1. **Identification:** Amsterdam/Dutch step-gabled house, 1600–1665 (Dutch Renaissance). Frontage about 5 m. Confidence: high.
2. **Comparables:** Brouwersgracht 264–268 (no. 17); Huis Bartolotti (no. 18); stepped houses in the Prinsengracht row (no. 15).
   - **Right:** a 2-bay narrow front, a stepped gable with a loading door and hoist beam at the top, and the gable windows narrowing upward (2 → 1 → door).
   - **Wrong:**
     - **Height.** 4 full storeys plus a basement plus a gable of 3 levels makes about 7 levels, roughly 23 m on a 5 m front. Real step-gabled houses are usually 2–3 storeys plus the gable. Built when step gables were, a 7-level house would be exceptional.
     - **Steps.** 7 steps per side, each about 0.7 m, are many small steps. Real gables typically have 3–5 larger steps, often with sandstone caps or pinnacles.
     - **Missing period signature.** There are no relieving arches over the windows, no cross-windows or shutters, and no sandstone banding. Each of these marks a pre-1665 front.
3. **Scores:** R 4, P 2. **Most damaging error:** proportions. The 7-level, 23 m height on a 5 m front, combined with tall sash windows, is an anachronistic mix.

### img11 — Amsterdam step gable, 3 bays
1. **Identification:** as img10, c.1600–1665, with later sash windows. Frontage about 6.5 m. Confidence: high.
2. **Comparables:** Brouwersgracht 264/266 (no. 17); Huis Bartolotti (no. 18).
   - **Right:** the 3-bay base. The gable windows step down 2 → 1 → loading door, and the hoist beam is on the axis.
   - **Wrong:** 6 small steps per side, and the same 7-level height problem as img10. The gable's internal vertical lines suggest pilasters with no capitals. There is no sandstone. The stoop has no landing.
3. **Scores:** R 4, P 3. **Most damaging error:** too many tiny steps on too tall a house.

### img14 — Amsterdam neck gable, 3 bays
1. **Identification:** as img00, c.1640–1770. Frontage about 7–7.5 m. Confidence: high.
2. **Comparables:** as img00 (nos. 14, 15, 16).
   - **Right:** the same correct parti as img00. The bel-etage windows are a little taller, which is good.
   - **Wrong:** the same bare brick claw-pieces, and a neck about 1.3 bays wide. The stoop is the same as img00.
   - In practice this is a near-duplicate of img00, so the generator varies little within this type.
3. **Scores:** R 5, P 3. **Most damaging error:** the bare brick shoulders, with no sandstone claw-pieces.

## Venetian Gothic family — shared observations

**Right in every Venetian Gothic drawing:**
- The tripartite composition: a central polifora over the *portego*, flanked by mono- or bifore.
- Several storeys: a ground floor with the water gate, an optional mezzanine, two *piani nobili* and an attic.
- Pink *marmorino*/stucco and thin string courses.
- Small *poggioli* under the side windows.
- An ogee water gate in a rectangular frame.
- The paired side windows in img03, img05 and img15, which match Palazzo Bernardo and Ca' Foscari.
- The double water gate in img15, which matches Pisani Moretta and Giustinian.

**Wrong in every Venetian Gothic drawing:**
- **(a) Arch shape.** The lights end in a smooth "bell", like a Gaussian curve, with no point. The Venetian ogee (*arco inflesso*) rises from a capital, reverses curvature and finishes in a **sharp point**, usually with a carved fleuron or finial. Higher-status windows add trefoil cusping. As drawn, the shapes read as Moorish or onion forms, not Venetian.
- **(b) Iron railings in place of stone balustrades.** On every Venetian Gothic palazzo I checked (Pisani Moretta, Ca' Foscari, Ca' d'Oro, Bernardo, Giustinian), the polifora sits on a **stone balustrade of colonnettes, projecting on stone corbels**. The drawings use iron railings set flush inside each light. Iron *poggioli* exist on minor houses and later alterations, but never under a principal polifora.
- **(c) No tracery** over the central polifora. Quatrefoils (Pisani Moretta, Ca' Foscari) or interlaced tracery (Ca' d'Oro) are common on the larger fronts. Their absence is acceptable on modest palazzi.
- **(d) Missing details.** There are no Istrian-stone corner quoins or rope mouldings. There is no dentil frame (*dentello*) around openings, apart from a square frame drawn behind some side windows.
- **(e) The lights are too wide.** They are about 1.3–1.6 m between columns, against roughly 0.9–1.2 m real. The columns are very thin, so the polifora reads as one glass wall.

### img02 — Venetian Gothic palazzo, 3-axis
1. **Identification:** Venice, Gothic (*gotico fiorito*), c.1400–1470, with a later attic. Frontage about 14–16 m. Confidence: high on city and style.
2. **Comparables:** Palazzo Contarini Fasan (no. 8); Ca' Loredan dell'Ambasciatore (no. 12); Palazzo Tiepolo (no. 11).
   - **Right:** a 4-light polifora on the first piano nobile and a 3-light one on the second, flanked by single monofore. Small *poggioli* under the side windows. A central water gate, and an attic of small square windows.
   - **Wrong:**
     - Bell arches with no point.
     - Iron railings under the polifora.
     - The polifora counts differ between floors (4 and 3) while keeping the same overall width, so the lights differ in size from floor to floor. Usually the same polifora repeats, or the upper one is smaller.
     - The ground-floor side windows are tiny round-headed openings. Real ones are rectangular, barred and larger.
3. **Scores:** R 4, P 3. **Most damaging error:** the pointless, bell-shaped arches.

### img03 — Venetian Gothic palazzo with mezzanine, paired side windows
1. **Identification:** Venice, Gothic, c.1420–1480. Frontage about 16–18 m. Confidence: high.
2. **Comparables:** Palazzo Bernardo (no. 10); Ca' Foscari (no. 6); Pisani Moretta (no. 5).
   - **Right:**
     - Paired monofore on each side with poggioli, and a 4- and 5-light polifora.
     - A mezzanine of small square windows above the ground floor. This is correct: the *ammezzato* is typical.
     - A row of small arched ground-floor windows beside the gate.
   - **Wrong:**
     - The bell arches and iron railings (common errors a and b).
     - The first piano nobile polifora (5 lights) is wider than the second (4 lights). The main polifora is normally on the *primo* piano nobile, so this is the right way round, but the column heights differ oddly.
     - The attic windows are evenly spaced and ignore the bay axes. That is plausible for a later attic.
3. **Scores:** R 4, P 3. **Most damaging error:** the iron railings in place of a corbelled stone balustrade under the polifore.

### img05 — large Venetian Gothic palazzo, 3 + 4/5 + 3 axes
1. **Identification:** Venice, Gothic, c.1430–1480, a large Grand Canal palace. Frontage about 20–24 m. Confidence: high.
2. **Comparables:** Ca' Foscari (no. 6); Palazzo Giustinian (no. 7); Pisani Moretta (no. 5).
   - **Right:** a big symmetrical front with three monofore each side and polifore on three upper floors. A mezzanine and a single central water gate.
   - **Wrong:**
     - Three stacked polifore (5, 4 and 4 lights) on three floors, with the top one drawn without a balcony. Even Ca' Foscari has polifore on only two floors.
     - Groups of three identical side monofore, evenly spaced, are unusual. Pairs are normal.
     - There are no tracery or quatrefoils, which a palace of this size would almost certainly have.
     - The common errors a and b also apply.
3. **Scores:** R 4, P 2. **Most damaging error:** a grand-palace scale with three polifore and no tracery or stone balustrades, inconsistent for its rank.

## Veneto-Byzantine family — shared observations

These are clearly modelled on the Fondaco dei Turchi and Ca' Loredan/Farsetti type: a ground arcade below and a continuous upper arcade (loggia) above.

**Right in every Byzantine drawing:**
- A ground arcade of large round arches below a lighter arcade with more, smaller arches.
- A light-coloured wall.
- Corner towers in img01 and img12, and crenellation (*merli*) in img12.

**Wrong in every Byzantine drawing:**
- **(a) Supports.** The arches rest on **fluted classical pilasters** with Renaissance capitals. The real buildings have free-standing marble **columns** with Byzantine basket or Corinthianesque capitals, carrying **stilted** arches with moulded extrados (see the Fondaco photo, no. 1). Fluting is a classical idiom that never appears here.
- **(b) Upper-loggia railings.** The upper loggia has an iron railing. The Fondaco and Ca' Farsetti have stone balustrades or carved *plutei*.
- **(c) No patere.** There are no *patere*, *formelle* or roundels in the spandrels, which are the signature ornament of the Veneto-Byzantine style (Fondaco and Ca' Loredan both have them).
- **(d) No arcade rhythm.** The upper arches do not relate to the lower ones. Counts of 11 over 6 (img04), 7 over 4 (img08) and 13 over 7 (img12) fall just short of 2:1, so piers land over arch crowns. The Fondaco has 18 over 10 and is also not exactly 2:1, so this is tolerable but looks careless.
- **(e) Missing upper floors.** These are 2-storey buildings with a flat cornice. The surviving examples (Loredan, Farsetti, da Mosto) almost all carry later upper floors. Only the restored Fondaco keeps the 2-storey-plus-towers form.

### img01 — Veneto-Byzantine fondaco with towers (no merlons)
1. **Identification:** Venice, Veneto-Byzantine *casa-fondaco*, 12th–13th c., modelled on the Fondaco dei Turchi. Frontage about 25–30 m. The real Fondaco is roughly 45–50 m, estimated from its 10-bay ground arcade. Confidence: high on the model.
2. **Comparables:** Fondaco dei Turchi (no. 1); Ca' Farsetti (no. 3); Ca' Loredan (no. 2).
   - **Right:**
     - Flanking towers 3 storeys high and slightly taller than the centre, with grouped triple and paired arched windows at the top.
     - A 5-arch ground arcade with a 9-arch upper loggia.
   - **Wrong:**
     - The towers have no crenellation. The Fondaco's merlons are its silhouette signature.
     - Each tower has a single large arched opening on its ground floor, where the Fondaco continues the arcade with 2–3 arches.
     - The common errors a–c also apply.
3. **Scores:** R 3, P 2. **Most damaging error:** fluted pilasters in place of columns carrying stilted arches.

### img04 — Veneto-Byzantine arcaded front, 2 storeys
1. **Identification:** Venice, Veneto-Byzantine, 12th–13th c. Frontage about 18–20 m. Confidence: medium. Without the towers it could also pass as a 19th-c. Italianate arcade or a Spanish-colonial *portal*.
2. **Comparables:** Ca' Farsetti (no. 3); Ca' Loredan (no. 2); the central body of the Fondaco (no. 1).
   - **Right:** a full-width ground portico of 6 round arches and an upper loggia of 11 arches.
   - **Wrong:**
     - The arcade runs edge to edge with no solid flanking wall. Loredan and Farsetti both frame the portico with solid bays pierced by windows.
     - There is a deep blank band between the floors.
     - The common errors a–e also apply.
3. **Scores:** R 2, P 2. **Most damaging error:** classical fluted pilasters and an iron railing with no patere, which removes every Byzantine signature.

### img08 — Veneto-Byzantine arcaded front, narrow
1. **Identification:** as img04. Frontage about 16–18 m. Confidence: medium-low.
2. **Comparables:** Ca' Loredan (no. 2) and Ca' Farsetti (no. 3).
   - **Right:** the arcade-over-arcade idea, with 4 ground arches and 7 upper arches.
   - **Wrong:**
     - The ground arches are very wide, about 4 m. The Fondaco's are about 3.5 m and Loredan's are narrower still.
     - The rest repeats img04.

### img12 — Veneto-Byzantine fondaco with towers and merlons
1. **Identification:** the Fondaco dei Turchi type, Venice, 13th c. as restored in the 1860s. Frontage about 28–32 m. Confidence: high.
2. **Comparables:** Fondaco dei Turchi (no. 1, plus the Naya photos showing the pre-restoration state); Ca' Farsetti (no. 3).
   - **Right:** this is the best of the four.
     - Towers with Venetian merlons: 4 per tower and a continuous row over the loggia.
     - A 7-arch ground arcade and a 13-arch upper loggia. The towers have one large arched window at ground level, two at the first floor and three at the top.
   - **Wrong:**
     - The merlons are drawn as tiny pointed tabs. Real *merli* are large and stepped, with a pointed top, about 1 m tall.
     - The common errors a–c also apply: pilasters, iron railing, no patere.
3. **Scores:** R 4, P 3. **Most damaging error:** fluted pilasters in place of columns carrying stilted arches.

## Summary across images

**Recurring errors, in order of damage:**
1. **Venetian ogee drawn as a smooth bell.** It has no point, finial or cusping, and appears in all six Gothic palazzi. An expert reads it as Moorish or onion, not Venetian. This is the cheapest fix with the biggest payoff.
2. **Iron railings everywhere.** They replace the Venetian Gothic polifora balustrade (stone colonnettes on corbels), the Byzantine loggia parapet (stone plutei), and the Amsterdam stoop railings (iron, which are missing outright). The material and position are inverted in every case.
3. **No signature stone ornament.** There are no sandstone claw-pieces or dressings in Amsterdam, no patere or roundels and no stilted columns in Byzantine Venice, and no tracery, quoins or dentil frames in Gothic Venice. The silhouettes are right, but the details that date a building are absent.
4. **Classical fluted pilasters as the default support** in the Byzantine arcades, which is the wrong order of architecture by about 300 years.
5. **Amsterdam stoop geometry.** The flight arrives beside the door with no landing. It is identical on all six houses.
6. **Proportion slips.**
   - Step-gable houses are too tall: 7 levels, with 6–7 tiny steps.
   - Window heights barely diminish upward.
   - Arcade counts on the two levels are unrelated.
   - Too many polifore are stacked on big palazzi.
   - Gate or loading-door axes do not match their functional partners (img06, img15).
7. **Low variety within a type.** img00 and img14 are near-identical. The Venetian Gothic set reuses one window, gate and balcony kit.

**Would pass as the real thing to an expert:**
- **At a glance:** img00, img14 (neck gables), img11 (step gable) and img13 (compact Gothic palazzo). The type, city and proportions are all right, and only the ornament is missing.
- **Recognised correctly but not convincing:** img02, 03, 09, 15, 12 and img10. The city and type are recognised immediately, but the arch shape, railing or proportion errors give them away within seconds.
- **Would not pass:** img04 and img08, which read as generic Mediterranean or Italianate arcades with no Byzantine evidence. Also img01, where pilasters, no merlons and no patere make it a "Victorian Italianate villa with towers". Also img06, because of the off-axis loading door, and img05, where the scale and ornament do not match. img07 is identifiable as Amsterdam only through the stoop.
