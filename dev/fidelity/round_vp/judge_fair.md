# Blind judgement, round VP (fair judge)

Judge: independent architectural historian, blind. I looked only at `images/img00.png` to `img15.png` at commit `d5a2ef2` and at the web. I opened no other file in the repository. The drawings are flat elevations with simplified flat colour, so I judged massing, rhythm, hierarchy and the presence or absence of characteristic elements, not rendering quality.

**About the reference photos.** All links are Wikimedia Commons file or category pages that I confirmed exist. The image host itself (thumb.wikimedia.org) was blocked from this machine, so I could not re-inspect every photo pixel by pixel. My comparisons rest on the documented character of these buildings and streets, which is well established, and not on a fresh close reading of each photograph.

## Scoring key

- **Recognisability (R), 1 to 5:** how fast and how surely an expert names the city and type. 5 means instantly and unambiguously.
- **Plausibility (P), 1 to 5:** whether this could be a real building of that place and date. 5 means nothing an expert would object to.
- **Expert at a glance:** YES (passes without comment), WITH FLAWS (identified correctly, but something jars), NO (rejected or misidentified).

## San Francisco images

### img00: front-gabled row house, bay left, door right

- **City / region:** San Francisco (Mission, Noe Valley or Western Addition), confidence 70%. It could just as easily be Oakland or Sacramento, because nothing in it is specific to SF except the stoop over a raised basement on a narrow lot.
- **Style / type:** front-gabled Stick or early Queen Anne row house, confidence 55%. The arched first-floor bay windows pull it back towards late Italianate.
- **Date:** c. 1885–1895, confidence 50%.
- **Frontage:** about 25 ft (7.6 m), one standard SF lot, confidence 75%.
- **Compared with:** the Mish House (Stick, 1885); Albert Wilford Houses (Queen Anne); Painted Ladies on Steiner St.
- **Right:** a narrow lot with a raised basement and a straight stoop up to a first-floor entry. A two-storey square bay beside a door bay, which is the standard SF two-bay plan. Horizontal rustic siding, corner boards, and a steep front gable.
- **Wrong:**
  - The gable is completely bare: no bargeboard, no shingle field, no sunburst or spindle panel, no raking cornice, and no frieze or brackets at the eave. Real SF gables are the most decorated part of the façade. This one reads as a farmhouse.
  - The bay stops at the eave line under a flat cornice that butts into the gable rake. In SF the bay normally either carries up into its own gable, or the whole front sits under a bracketed cornice with the gable above it. The collision looks unresolved.
  - The door has no hood, portico or entry porch, only a transom.
  - The roof is wider than the house on both sides, which suggests a detached house, not a row house on a lot with zero side setbacks.
- **Most damaging error:** the undecorated gable meeting the bay cornice.
- **R 3 / P 3 / WITH FLAWS**

### img01: Italianate, door left, slanted bay right (pink)

- **City / region:** San Francisco, confidence 90%.
- **Style / type:** Italianate row house with a false front, bracketed cornice and two-storey slanted bay, confidence 90%.
- **Date:** c. 1872–1882, confidence 70%.
- **Frontage:** about 25 ft, confidence 80%.
- **Compared with:** Sloss-Lilienthal House (1876); 1249–1251 Scott St; the Pacific Heights slanted bays.
- **Right:** the false front with a bracketed cornice and frieze. A three-sided slanted bay with narrow side lights and colonnettes. Tall round- and segmental-headed 2-over-2 sashes with keystone hoods. A stoop over a raised basement. This is the core SF Italianate, and the rhythm is correct.
- **Wrong:**
  - There is no entry portico. The SF Italianate entrance is almost always a recessed porch framed by columns or pilasters carrying an entablature or pediment, with a transom over double doors. Here there is a bare door with a keystone.
  - A strip of plain wall sits between the frieze and the cornice brackets. On real buildings the brackets sit directly on the panelled frieze.
  - The bay begins at the first floor without any visible support. Most SF slanted bays rise from the ground through the basement. Where they start higher, they sit on brackets or a corbelled base.
  - The brackets are evenly spaced single blocks. Paired scroll brackets over a panelled frieze are the norm.
- **Most damaging error:** the missing entry portico.
- **R 4 / P 4 / WITH FLAWS**

## Paris images

### img04: five bays, R+3, porte cochère, plain box dormers

- **City / region:** Paris, confidence 85%.
- **Style / type:** a modest immeuble de rapport. It is late Louis-Philippe or early Second Empire in height and treatment, with Haussmannian balcony rules applied. Confidence 55%.
- **Date:** c. 1845–1865, confidence 45%.
- **Frontage:** five bays, about 14–16 m, confidence 70%.
- **Compared with:** 14 and 34 rue Notre-Dame-de-Lorette, and 27 place Saint-Georges (low, regular pre-Haussmann façades); 16 rue de Turbigo.
- **Right:** a stone (pierre de taille) façade. A continuous balcony and small pediments on the étage noble. Balconettes (garde-corps) on the floor above. A zinc mansard with dormers aligned on the bays, chimney stacks on the party walls, and an arched porte cochère in an end bay. The rhythm is correct.
- **Wrong:**
  - The dormers are plain rectangles punched into the roof, with no cap, pediment or cheeks. A Parisian lucarne is always a small framed structure.
  - The ground floor and entresol have no rustication (refends). Haussmannian and late Louis-Philippe bases are almost always channelled.
  - The roof cornice is a thin band. It should be a strong moulded, modillioned cornice that carries a balcony gutter line.
  - There are no consoles under the noble-floor balcony.
  - A full mansard on an R+3 of this date is plausible, but with Haussmannian balcony logic and only three storeys, the date sits uneasily.
- **Most damaging error:** the box dormers.
- **R 4 / P 3 / WITH FLAWS**

## Recurring errors

Ranked by how much they cost at a glance.

1. **San Francisco entrances have no portico (all 8 SF images).** The recessed entry porch with columns and an entablature or pediment is a defining feature of the SF Italianate front, and it is present on Stick houses too. Every SF door here is a bare door, at best with a small pediment. This is the single most frequent thing an SF expert would notice.
2. **Gables are bare (img00, 08, 09).** The front-gable houses have no bargeboard, shingle field, sunburst, stickwork or eave cornice. Without them, the gable houses lose their city and style and fall to R3. The bay cornice also collides with the gable rake, and the roof overhangs the side walls as if the house were detached.
3. **Paris roofs are oversized (img10, 12, and to a lesser degree 06).** Two-tier mansards that are 1.4 to 2.2 storeys tall sit on façades that are otherwise 1860–80 in character. This is the error most likely to produce a NO in Paris.
4. **There is no storey hierarchy in Paris (all 8 Paris images).** The 2nd (noble) floor should be the tallest, with heights falling upwards. Here all upper storeys are equal. The balcony scheme is right, but the proportional signal underneath it is missing.
5. **Paris bases are not rusticated, and roof cornices are thin (all Paris).** The channelled RDC and entresol, and the strong modillioned cornice under the roof, are the frame of a Haussmannian façade. Both are absent or reduced to a line. Balcony consoles are also missing.
6. **Paris entrances are underplayed on large buildings (img05, 06, 10).** A single window-sized door serves fronts of 5 to 8 bays. Wide buildings need a porte cochère or a strongly framed entrance, and on wide streets a commercial ground floor.
7. **Bay logic in San Francisco.** In img07 the bay stops a storey below the cornice, which drives the only SF NO. Bays across the set have no base or support where they start above the basement. In img09 the arched windows are on the upper floor and square ones on the parlour floor.
8. **Dormers are drawn flat (img04, 14).** Box dormers with no caps, and oculi drawn as holes, make the roof read as a wall.
9. **Minor, SF:** a plain band between the frieze and the cornice brackets, evenly spaced single brackets instead of pairs, and no quoins.

**What works consistently.** The type identification is excellent. The SF lot width, raised basement, stoop, false front and slanted-bay rhythm are right. The Paris bay alignment, balcony distribution, mansard with aligned dormers, party-wall chimneys and shopfront / porte cochère grammar are right. Fixing items 1, 3 and 4 alone would move most WITH FLAWS ratings to YES.
