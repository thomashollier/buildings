## Overall read

The set reads immediately as **New York, SoHo / Cast Iron Historic District, c. 1855–1880**. Two families:

- **Cast-iron lofts** (00, 01, 02, 06, 07, 08, 13, 14, 15): piers with capitals, floor-by-floor entablatures, arched or square-headed bays, a bracketed sheet-metal cornice, and a glazed iron storefront. These are the strong ones (R4).
- **Masonry lofts and walk-ups** (03, 04, 05, 09, 10, 11, 12): brick or painted/stuccoed walls with punched windows, fire escapes and water tanks. These read as "Manhattan walk-up", but not specifically SoHo, and they are where most of the giveaways sit (R3, P2).

No drawing would pass an expert with no flaw noticed. 14 comes closest.

## Per drawing

R = recognisability (1–5), P = plausibility (1–5).

**img00**: NYC SoHo, cast-iron store and loft, 6 bays, 6 storeys, c. 1865–70. **R4 P3 WITH FLAWS.**
- The arched bays have no columns. Every real arcaded SoHo front (A, D) stands a Corinthian column or colonnette on each pier, carrying the arch; here the arches float on a flat, fluted pilaster strip.
- Cornice brackets are spaced evenly at about 0.37 of a bay, ignoring the piers. In A and B a large console sits over every pier, with smaller modillions between, and there is a dentil course and frieze.
- The floor entablatures are thin lines. In A, B and D each floor has a cornice that projects and casts a shadow, and it is the strongest horizontal on the façade.
- The ground floor is about 1.07× an upper storey and sits flush on the sidewalk. It has no transom, no shop door and no loading step.

**img01**: NYC SoHo, wide cast-iron loft, 9 bays and 4 storeys, with a segmental name-plate pediment, c. 1860s. **R4 P3 WITH FLAWS.**
- The pediment is good (compare D's segmental date panel), but on a 9-bay front a centre pavilion or end-pier emphasis is expected (B). Nine identical bays look extruded.
- The top flight of the fire escape runs diagonally from the cornice down to the top balcony. On real escapes, the top balcony reaches the roof by a vertical gooseneck ladder (A, C).
- Eight storefront bays with no shop door is not possible for a working store.
- Four storeys is low for a 9-bay iron front; 5–6 is the norm (A, F).

**img02**: NYC SoHo, cast-iron loft, 6 bays, 5 storeys. **R4 P3 WITH FLAWS.**
- Same issues as 00: no columns, a thin floor entablature, brackets unrelated to the piers, a short ground floor and no shop door.
- The fire escape (top flight again from the cornice) and the drop ladder are well placed.

**img03**: NYC, masonry (painted brick or stucco) loft or tenement, 7 bays, 6 storeys, triangular pediment, c. 1870–90. **R3 P2 NO.**

- The windows are small for a loft. The glass is about 22% of the wall, against about 35–45% in C and E.

**img05**: NYC, red-brick loft, 7 bays, 4 storeys. **R3 P2 NO.**

**img14**: NYC SoHo, 3-bay arcaded cast-iron loft, 6 storeys, c. 1857–65 (Haughwout family). **R4 P4 WITH FLAWS. Best of the set.**

## Top giveaways across the set, ranked, with fixes

1. **Masonry windows have no lintels or sills (03, 04, 05, 09, 10, 11, 12; 7 of 16).** This is the instant "not real" for every brick or painted building.
   *Fix:* every masonry opening gets a projecting lintel and sill. Choose a lintel type per building (not per window): flat stone cap 35%, bracketed or pedimented iron hood 30% (C, E), segmental-arched hood 20%, eared architrave 15%. Make the lintel height 0.20–0.28 × the window width and project it 0.04–0.08 × the width beyond each jamb. Make the sill 0.08 × the width tall, extending the same distance as the lintel. Optionally make the hoods on the lowest upper floor one grade richer (C does this).

2. **Storefronts have no shop door, transom or storefront entablature (all 16).** A run of 2–8 glazed bays with no entrance is impossible.
   *Fix:* ground-floor height = 1.35–1.5 × an upper storey (currently about 1.05–1.15). Split each storefront bay into a transom band (top 18–22%, multi-light), main glass (about 65%) and a bulkhead (12–15%). Each shop gets one door, recessed 1–1.5 m, in the bay nearest the centre of that shop: 1 door per 2–3 bays. Put a storefront entablature with a dentil course (8–10% of ground-floor height) above everything (C, E). Align all door heads with the transom bar.

3. **Masonry ground floors lack cast-iron columns (03, 04, 05, 09–12).** In SoHo, brick upper floors almost always stand on an iron storefront.
   *Fix:* reuse the cast-iron storefront module under every masonry building: Corinthian or fluted columns at every bay, entablature over. Use pilasters only at the two party walls.

4. **Floor entablatures on cast-iron fronts are too thin (00, 01, 02, 06, 07, 08, 13, 14, 15).** The defining look of A, B and D is a stack of strongly projecting floor cornices.
   *Fix:* floor entablature depth = 0.10–0.12 × the storey height (currently about 0.04), in three parts: architrave, plain frieze, and a projecting cornice with dentils. The top member should overhang the piers by about 0.3 × the pier width.

5. **The bays have no columns; piers are flat fluted strips (all cast iron except partly 14).**
   *Fix:* on 50% of cast-iron fronts, stand an engaged Corinthian column in front of each pier, rising from the floor entablature to the next one. The arch springs from its capital (A) or a smaller colonnette (14). For the rest, keep piers but give them a real capital silhouette: necking, flared bell and a square abacus about 1.3 × the pier width. End piers should be 1.5–2 × the width of interior piers, or paired columns (B, D).

6. **Cornice brackets ignore the bay rhythm (all 16).** Evenly spaced identical wedges read as a pattern, not as construction.
   *Fix:* place one large console over every pier (doubled at the ends). Add 1–3 modillions between consoles (more for wider bays), a dentil course under the corona, and a frieze with recessed panels between consoles. Console height = 1.3–1.6 × the frieze height. Use the same bracket shape on at most a third of the buildings.

7. **Fire escape top flight is a stair from the cornice (01, 02, 06, 07, 08, 09–13, 15).**
   *Fix:* draw stairs only between balconies, at 45–55° within the balcony length. From the top balcony draw a vertical gooseneck ladder over the parapet. Add triangular brackets under each balcony (C). Use a drop ladder or counterweighted stair from the lowest balcony. Fire escapes on about 40–50% of buildings (not about 75% as here), and prefer 2–4 bays.

8. **The ground floor sits flush on the sidewalk (all 16).**
   *Fix:* raise the storefront floor 0.4–1.0 m on a loading platform or vault-light band, with 1–3 steps at doors. This applies to about 60% of buildings (E shows the platforms).

9. **Hairlines on masonry walls (floor lines 03, 04, 10, 11; vertical bay lines 05, 09, 12).**
   *Fix:* remove them. If a horizontal is wanted, use a real stone belt course at the second floor only, or sill-course bands. Never use vertical lines.

10. **Water tanks (09, 10, 13).**
    *Fix:* draw a hooped cylinder (diameter about 0.25–0.35 × the building width, height about 1.1 × the diameter) with a conical roof (pitch about 30°). Stand it on a steel frame of 4–6 legs, 2–4 m tall, set back so the bottom third is hidden behind the parapet. Use one on about 30% of buildings and vary its left/right position.

11. **Mismatched ground-floor doors (10, 12, 13).**
    *Fix:* use one residential door per building, single or narrow double (about 1.1–1.5 m), with a transom. Shop doors are glazed and recessed. All door heads align with the storefront transom bar.

12. **Repetition across the set.** The same diamond spandrel panel is on all nine cast-iron fronts, the same bracket wedge on all 16, and the same tank three times.
    *Fix:* spandrel options: plain raised panel 45%, balustrade 25% (A), diamond 15%, fluted or blank 15%. Masonry storey heights should shrink 4–7% per floor going up (C, E); cast-iron fronts can stay uniform. On 7+ bay fronts, give 30% a centre pavilion or end-bay emphasis (B).

If fixes 1–5 alone were applied, I would expect the masonry set to move from P2 to P3 and the cast-iron set from P3 to P4.
