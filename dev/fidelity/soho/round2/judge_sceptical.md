## Summary

The set is unmistakably SoHo / Lower Manhattan: cast-iron loft fronts over full-width shopfronts, a single stair-hall door at one end, bracketed cornices and street-side fire escapes. A lay viewer would accept most of them. No drawing passes a specialist. The iron fronts (11 of 16) are the strongest. The four tan masonry fronts are the weakest because their openings have no lintels or sills. Recurring faults: the ground floor is barely taller than the floors above, the ground floor has no storefront cornice, iron columns or loading platform, the fire-escape stairs are hairlines that climb up into the cornice, and every iron front uses the same lozenge spandrel.

## Per drawing

### img00: arcaded iron front, 6 bays
- **Arches do not work structurally or visually.** Each arch is a blue half-disc sitting on top of a rectangular 2/2 sash, and the archivolt springs from bare wall. The pier capitals are trapezoids at the top of the storey, well above the springing line. At Haughwout (A) each arch springs from the capital of an engaged Corinthian colonette, the upper sash itself is round-headed, and there is a keystone.
- **Piers are flat, fluted strips with no order.** Iron fronts are columnar: ¾-round or engaged columns with bases, Corinthian or Composite capitals, and an entablature at each floor (A, B, C). Here each floor line is a plain band with no dentils or modillions.
- **No hierarchy.** All five upper storeys are identical and the end piers are the same width as the intermediate ones. Real fronts thicken the end piers, often with quoins or rustication (B, C), and usually change the top or second storey.
- **The ground floor is too low.** It is about 1.14 × an upper storey (about 217 px against 190 px). Real fronts are 1.3–1.5 × (A, D).
- The shopfront has two doors of different widths at the two ends. That is plausible, but the wide left door is a domestic panelled pair. A loft would have a freight door or a glazed iron-framed door.

### img01: 9 bays, 3 + ground, segmental parapet
- Same arch fault as 00. The single-pane sash with one meeting rail reads as a 20th-century replacement, which is plausible, but a sceptic notes it.
- **Fire escape.** The stair from the top balcony climbs up to the cornice. Real fire escapes end at the top balcony with a vertical gooseneck ladder over the parapet (F, A). The stairs are single hairlines, where real stairs are a stringer pair about 0.6 m wide with visible treads (G). The balconies have no brackets underneath.
- **A 9-bay front with one 3-bay fire escape at one end** is thin coverage. Long fronts such as B and E typically carry two.
- The segmental parapet with a blank name panel is credible (cf. B's pediment), but it sits on a cornice with no frieze. Real cornices carry a panelled frieze with paired brackets over each column.
- Ground floor about 1.16 × an upper storey, again too low.

### img02: 6 bays, 4 + ground
- Same arch, pier and hierarchy faults as 00.
- The fire escape over 2 of 6 bays is plausible. The top stair rising into the cornice is wrong.
- The drop ladder hangs in front of a shop window. That happens in reality, but an expert would expect it aligned on a pier.

### img03: tan masonry, 7 bays
- **Punched openings with no lintels, no sills and no reveals.** This is the single biggest giveaway in the set. Every 19th-century SoHo brick front has stone or cast-iron lintels, often pedimented or eared (E, left building, dated 1860), plus projecting sills.
- **A drawn line at every floor** across a flat wall. Brick fronts have no such joints. At most there is one belt course, at the second floor.
- The windows are small and sparse, so the wall reads as stucco infill, not a loft. Lofts have big openings, 45–60 % glazing per bay.
- The pediment over a plain wall has no structural or decorative logic.
- The base is a flat panel with strip piers, with no iron columns, capitals or storefront cornice.

### img04: narrow tan masonry, 3 bays
- Same no-lintel and floor-line faults as 03.
- The windows are not centred in the bay grid. The right window sits much nearer the edge than the left window does. That is plausible on a converted house, but it reads as accidental.
- The ground floor has a wide domestic double door and two shop windows, with no iron columns.

### img05: brick store, 7 bays × 3
- The proportions are good. Low, wide, regular 7-bay rhythm, as on early-19th-c. brick stores on Broome or Spring.
- Openings have only a thin pale surround. Greek Revival brick has plain stone lintels and sills (≈ 0.15 × window width deep). Italianate brick has hooded lintels (E).
- The faint vertical lines between bays read as piers that are not there. Plain Flemish-bond fronts have no pier joints.
- The fire escape and its top stair into the cornice have the same faults as 01.

### img06: segmental-headed iron front, 9 bays
- The segmental heads are thin blue caps over flat-headed frames. On C the frame and sash follow the segment, and the head carries an arched hood or moulding.
- The lozenge spandrel repeated 36 times, the flat pilasters and the absence of an entablature make it much flatter than C, which reads as a dense grid of columns and cornices.
- The fire escape is centred on bays 4–5 of 9, which is credible. The railings are drawn behind the spandrel panel, so the balusters vanish halfway. That is a z-order error visible at full size.

### img07: narrow iron front, 3 bays × 5
- Good narrow-lot massing (cf. D), credible.
- The fire escape spans the full width with stairs alternating correctly, but the top flight runs up into the cornice.
- **The entry door is a dark recess, and the drop ladder lands right on it.** Real drop ladders are set over the sidewalk clear of the door swing.
- Flat pilasters, no capitals at the ground floor, no storefront cornice.

### img08: narrow iron front, 3 bays × 4
- Credible proportions and good rhythm.
- The spandrel panels are a contrasting colour, but they are still the same lozenge.
- The ground floor (door plus two shop windows) is believable. The ground-storey height is too close to the upper storeys.

### img09: brick 3-bay with water tank
- **The water tank is undersized.** It is about 0.27 of the façade width. On a 25-ft lot that is about 7 ft across and 6 ft tall. Real wood tanks are 10–16 ft in diameter, with height ≈ 1–1.2 × diameter (see the tank on A). The tank also has no hoops, and its roof reads as a gable. It should be a shallow cone.
- The windows have no lintels or sills, only a thin surround.
- The vertical pier lines in the brick are spurious, as on 05.

### img10: tan masonry 3-bay with water tank
- The lintel-less openings and the line at every floor make it read as a modern stucco re-face.
- The tank is undersized, as on 09.
- The ground floor (wide door, one window, narrow door) is plausible.

### img11: tan masonry 7-bay
- Same faults as 03. The segmental parapet on a featureless wall looks applied.

### img12: brick 3-bay with triangular pediment
- **A full triangular pediment above a bracketed cornice on a plain 3-bay brick front is a stylistic non-sequitur.** Sheet-metal cornices on such buildings have a raised central name panel or a small segmental crest, not a temple pediment.
- No lintels or sills.
- The full-width fire escape is credible on a tenement.

### img13: narrow iron front with water tank
- Good, but the tank is undersized as on 09. The legs sit directly on the cornice line. A real tank stands set back on a steel frame 2–3 m high.
- The drop ladder lands on the right-hand door.

### img14: narrow arcaded iron front, 4 + ground
- **The best of the set.** Small impost blocks at the springing finally give the arches something to spring from. The proportions are close to narrow Broadway and Broome arcaded fronts. There is no fire escape clutter, and the recessed entry is a good touch.
- The pier capitals still sit at the top of the storey, not at the springing. The arches still sit over rectangular sash. There is no keystone and no ground-floor order, and the ground floor is only ≈ 1.06 × an upper storey.

### img15: narrow iron front, 3 + ground, very wide bays
- The windows fill about 85 % of the bay, wider than almost any SoHo iron front, where glazing is 55–70 % of the bay because the columns have real width (D).
- The full-width fire escape is credible. The railings are hidden behind the spandrels (z-order) and the top stair runs into the cornice.

## Top giveaways across all 16, ranked, with fixes

1. **Masonry openings with no lintels or sills** (03, 04, 05, 09, 10, 11, 12: 7 of 16). Fix: every masonry window gets a lintel 0.12–0.18 × window width tall, projecting 2–4 % of the bay width, and a sill 0.05–0.07 × window width. Choose the style by date: plain stone slab (Greek Revival, 30 %), cornice-headed or eared hood (Italianate, 50 %), or segmental brick arch with keystone (20 %). Remove the thin pale surround.
2. **A drawn line at every floor on masonry walls** (03, 04, 10, 11). Fix: delete them. At most, add a single belt course at the second-floor sill (50 %) and a cornice-line frieze. Also delete the vertical pier hairlines in brick (05, 09, 12).
3. **The ground floor is too low and has no storefront structure** (all 16). Fix: ground storey 1.35–1.5 × the typical upper storey. Add a projecting storefront entablature with modillions or dentils at the second-floor line, about 0.12 × the ground-storey height. Ground piers become cast-iron columns or pilasters with base and capital (Corinthian 50 %, Doric/neo-Grec 50 %). Add a raised vault-light loading platform, 0.5–0.9 m high, across 60–80 % of fronts, with a step and an iron edge.
4. **Fire-escape top flight runs up into the cornice** (all 12 fire escapes). Fix: the stairs run only between balconies. From the top balcony a vertical gooseneck ladder rises through or over the cornice to the roof. Draw each stair as a two-stringer band about 0.6 m wide with treads every 0.2 m, not as a hairline. Add triangular brackets under each balcony. Draw the railing in front of the spandrel panel, never behind it. Place the drop ladder clear of any door (offset ≥ 1 bay or on a pier). On fronts of 7 or more bays, use two fire escapes about 50 % of the time.
5. **The arches are not tectonic** (00, 01, 02, 14). Fix: put the column or pilaster capital, or an impost block, exactly at the springing line, and spring the arch from it. Give the sash a round head that follows the arch. Add a keystone (80 %). Make the piers engaged ¾ columns with Corinthian capitals on pedestals, as on Haughwout (A).
6. **Flat pilasters and no entablatures at the upper floors** (all iron fronts). Fix: each floor line becomes an entablature (architrave, frieze, cornice) projecting about 1.5–2 % of the façade width, with dentils on 50 %. Use ¾-round columns on 50 % of iron fronts and panelled piers on the rest (C).
7. **No hierarchy across the front.** Fix: make the end piers 1.5–2 × the width of the intermediate piers, with quoins or rustication on 40 %. On fronts of 7 or more bays, emphasise the centre (B) 30 % of the time with a central pediment, wider centre bays or paired columns. Make the top storey 0.9 × the height of the others on 50 %, or diminish 3–5 % per floor.
8. **The same lozenge spandrel everywhere** (all 11 iron fronts). Fix: pick per building from plain recessed panel (35 %), lozenge (20 %), rosette or paterae (20 %), balustrade (15 %, as on A) and dentil strip (10 %).
9. **Water tanks are undersized and crude** (09, 10, 13). Fix: diameter 0.4–0.55 × the width of a 25-ft front (10–14 ft), height 1–1.2 × diameter, on a steel frame 2–3 m high set back from the cornice, 3–5 hoops visible, shallow conical roof with a finial. Show them on about 40 % of buildings, not only on 3-bay ones.
10. **Window-to-bay ratio is too high on narrow fronts** (15, partly 07 and 13). Fix: glazing width 55–70 % of the bay. Glazing height 70–80 % of the storey on iron fronts and 50–60 % on masonry.
11. **Pediments with no logic** (03, 12). Fix: on plain masonry fronts use a raised central name panel or a small segmental crest in the sheet-metal cornice. Reserve the triangular pediment for iron fronts of 5 or more bays with a centre emphasis.

## What already convinces
- The single stair-hall door at one end of a shopfront is right for SoHo lofts.
- The bay counts (3, 6, 7 and 9 bays) and storey counts (4–6 total) match real lot widths and heights.
- The 2/2 sash, the fire escape on a 2–3 bay balcony and the bracketed cornices are all correct period vocabulary.
- img14 shows that one small tectonic fix (imposts) raises plausibility by a full point.
