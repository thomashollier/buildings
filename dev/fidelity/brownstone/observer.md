# Brownstone rowhouses, NYC 1840–1900: observer notes (round 1)

Independent observation from photographs and one HABS drawing set. No project code, tests or docs were
opened. Scale estimates use: stoop riser ≈ 0.19–0.23 m, upper window opening ≈ 1.0–1.1 m wide, door
leaf ≈ 2.4 m, person 1.7 m, and the HABS dimensions below as the one hard anchor. Confidence: **H**
(measured or read off a drawing), **M** (several photos agree), **L** (one oblique photo, or prior
knowledge not confirmed in a photo here).

Limits: Commons rate-limited the API hard, so the sample leans on what the first searches returned. No NYC
Landmarks designation report was opened this round, and the only measured drawing is HABS NY-449 (4 Grove
St), which predates the brownstone era slightly but fixes the plan and storey heights of the type.

## 2. Hard numbers from HABS NY-449 (4 Grove St), confidence H

- Storey heights, floor to floor: basement **8'-4" (2.54 m)**, parlour **10'-4" (3.15 m)**, second **9'-3" (2.82 m)**, attic to ridge 10'-0". Parlour ≈ 1.1× upper.
- Plan: house **≈ 22–23 ft (6.8 m)** wide × **30'-9" (9.4 m)** deep main block; front parlour 15'-3" × 14'-0", side hall 4'-0" clear. Side-hall plan = door in the outer bay.
- Front: 3 bays; front door opening **3'-0" × 7'-8" (0.91 × 2.34 m)**, parlour windows **3'-0" × 6'-5" (0.91 × 1.96 m)**, upper windows ~2'-10" × 6'-10". Window h/w ≈ 2.1–2.4.
- Stoop: **4–5 risers** to a parlour floor about 0.9–1.0 m above the sidewalk; wrought-iron handrail with urn/knob newels on a short iron post. Basement half-sunk behind a low iron areaway fence.
- This is the low-stoop baseline that the brownstone era raised.

## 3. TYPICAL (seen in most of the 1850–1890 brownstone rows)

**Massing and rhythm**
- 3 storeys over a raised basement (≈ 70% of rows seen); 4 over basement in Harlem and the UWS (#10, #24). Flat roof behind a cornice; no visible roof. (M)
- **3 bays per house**, door in an outer bay (side-hall plan), so a row reads door–window–window | door–window–window. Neighbours are often **mirrored in pairs**, putting two doors and two stoops side by side (#10, #6, #19). (M)
- House width **≈ 5.5–7.6 m (18–25 ft)**; 20 ft (6.1 m) is the commonest lot. Bay pitch ≈ 1.9–2.2 m centre to centre; window opening ≈ 1.0–1.1 m wide, so solid pier ≈ window width. (M)
- One continuous cornice height and one continuous sill/lintel line along a speculative row of 4–12 identical houses; a row is broken only where a new builder starts, with a step in cornice height of ≈ 0.3–1 m (#9, #10, #24). On slopes the whole row steps (#2). (M)

**Storey heights (photo estimates, scaled by window width and risers)**
- Sidewalk to parlour floor **≈ 2.0–2.8 m** (10–13 risers). (M)
- Parlour floor to floor **≈ 3.6–4.3 m**; upper floors **≈ 3.0–3.6 m**, decreasing upward. (L–M)
- Window heights fall by storey: parlour ≈ 2.3–2.6 m (sill only ≈ 0.4–0.6 m above floor, near floor-length), second ≈ 2.0–2.1 m, top ≈ 1.6–1.8 m. This graded diminution is one of the strongest visual signatures. (M)

**The stoop**
- Straight run perpendicular to the façade, **10–13 risers**, ≈ 1.5–1.8 m wide, landing ≈ 1.2–1.5 m deep at the parlour door. (M)
- Basement entrance **under the stoop**, through a low (≈ 2 m) arched or square opening with an iron gate. (H; seen in nearly every Italianate/neo-Grec photo)
- Italianate/neo-Grec balustrade: **cast iron**, heavy turned or vase-shaped balusters (≈ 15 cm pitch), a moulded rail, and a big **cast-iron newel at the foot** (≈ 1.1–1.3 m tall, square, urn- or ball-topped, incised in neo-Grec; #6, #7, #9, #14). Painted black or dark green. (H)
- Stone side walls (cheek walls) under the iron balustrade, stepping with the treads, in the same stone as the base. (M)

**Areaway and basement**
- Basement storey **half sunk**: its floor ≈ 0.6–1.0 m below sidewalk, windows sit just above grade and are barred. Basement face is rusticated (channelled) brownstone or plain stone. (M)
- Areaway ≈ 1.5–3 m deep in front of the basement, enclosed by a **cast-iron fence ≈ 0.9–1.1 m** on a low stone curb, with spear or fleur-de-lis finials, running house to house and returning to the stoop newel (#6, #7, #8). (H)

**Doorways**
- Italianate: **round-arched double doors** (paired leaves ≈ 0.65–0.75 m each, opening ≈ 1.3–1.5 m × 3.0–3.3 m including the arched head), deeply recessed in a vestibule, framed by a projecting enframement: pilasters or console brackets carrying a segmental or triangular **hood**; heavy foliated consoles on the best rows (#6, #8). (H)
- Neo-Grec: same proportions but square-headed or flat-arched, with **incised (machine-cut) linear ornament**, angular consoles and a flat, stepped hood (#9, #25). (M)
- Door top and parlour window tops align; door often slightly taller. (M)

**Window lintels and hoods**
- Every window gets a **projecting hood**: Italianate = segmental or flat cornice on small consoles; neo-Grec = flat stepped lintel with incised grooves and "ears". Projection ≈ 10–20 cm. Sills project ≈ 8–12 cm on small brackets. (H)
- Two-over-two sash (originals), often with an arched-top upper sash on the parlour floor. (M)

**Cornice**
- **Bracketed cornice** at the roof, ≈ 0.6–1.0 m deep, projecting ≈ 0.5–0.8 m: paired or single scroll brackets at ≈ 0.6–1.0 m spacing, a frieze with panels or small attic windows between them, and a moulded crown. Usually **pressed sheet metal or wood painted dark** (brown, black, green), so it reads darker than the stone. (H)

**Material and colour**
- Brownstone ashlar veneer, smooth-dressed, in **chocolate to reddish-brown** (#7, #8, #10), and rosy or pink-brown where weathered or refaced (#13, #17). Joints barely visible. Many are now painted or stuccoed over in the same colour range. (H)
- Ironwork black or dark green; doors varnished oak or walnut; window frames dark. (M)

## 4. VARIES

- **Bays.** Full-height **three-sided angled bays** (basement to cornice; the cornice follows the bay plan) in about a third of Brooklyn rows (#7, #12, #13); **round bowfronts** in 1890s rows (#21, #13, #24). Rows often alternate flat house / bay house, or bay / entrance / bay (#12). A bay is ≈ 3.0–3.6 m wide and projects ≈ 0.9–1.2 m. (M)
- **Oriels.** Corbelled oriels starting at the 2nd floor, over a parlour window, on carved brackets (#20, #23, #24, #5). (M)
- **Façade material.** Brick fronts with brownstone trim are as common as full brownstone, especially before c.1855 and in the 1890s. Red brick in Brooklyn Heights (#2, #4), Roman/buff brick banded with brownstone in Park Slope Romanesque (#19, #20). (M)
- **Ornament level** varies a lot even in one row (see §6).
- **Painted façades.** Cream, white, pale grey or pink paint on individual houses breaks a brownstone row (#4 of #13, #12, #14). Modern, but now typical of the street scene. (M)
- **Stoop orientation.** Mostly straight; Romanesque houses sometimes have L-shaped or side-entry stoops with landings (L; prior knowledge, not clearly seen here).
- **Storey count.** 2½ (with dormers) in the earliest rows, 3 over basement normally, 4 over basement in later Manhattan rows.

## 5. RARE

- **Colonnade of two-storey square piers** carrying a full entablature across a whole row, with no stoops (#3, Colonnade Row, Willow Place, 1846).
- **Continuous wooden or stone veranda** across a pair, on square piers, with a low stoop (#11, Clinton Ave).
- **Columned entrance porticos** on each stoop landing, carrying an entablature (#10, Lenox Ave).
- **Stepped (Flemish) gable** (#18) and **steep front gables with carved panels** (#23); **tile hip roofs with dormers** (#22). All Romanesque/Queen Anne, c.1888–95.
- **American basement / no stoop**, with the entrance one or two steps above grade (#23, #26). In this sample it appears after c.1890.
- Rock-faced (quarry-faced) ashlar across a whole façade (#25, W 76th St).

## 6. Correlations

- **Date → stoop height.** Pre-1845: 4–7 risers (#1, #2). 1850–1885: 10–13 risers, the highest stoops. 1890s: either high stoops with **solid stone cheek walls** and iron handrails only (#19, #20, #17) or low/no stoop (#23, #26, #16). (M)
- **Date → material.** 1840s: brick with plain brownstone slab lintels and sills, Greek Revival door frame of flat pilasters + entablature (#2). 1850s–80s: full brownstone fronts. Late 1880s–90s: polychrome — brick + brownstone banding, rock-faced stone at the base, terracotta panels, limestone (#18–21). (M)
- **Date → ornament.** Greek Revival: plain, flat, rectilinear; the door frame is the only ornament. Italianate (1850–75): round arches, heavy foliated consoles, segmental hoods, bracketed cornice. Neo-Grec (1870–85): same massing, ornament flattened and incised, angular brackets, square-headed openings. Romanesque (1885–95): round arches springing from short columns, rock-faced base, carved foliate friezes as a continuous band over the parlour, arcaded top storey, asymmetry within the house. (M)
- **Date → cornice.** Wood/metal bracketed cornice dominates 1850–1885; Romanesque swaps it for a corbelled brick or modillioned copper cornice (green, #19), or a gable. (M)
- **Width/wealth → bays and ornament.** Wider and richer houses (≈ 7–7.6 m, 25 ft) carry bays, oriels, carved enframements and 4 storeys; speculative 16–18 ft houses are flat, 3 bays (sometimes 2), with stock hoods and a cornice shared along the row. (L–M)
- **Row rhythm.** Speculative rows are identical; architect-designed 1890s rows deliberately vary each house (A-B-A-C) while holding a shared cornice and stoop height (#17, #19, #24). (M)

## 7. What would most make a model read as "brownstone"

1. Raised parlour floor with a long, straight, ≈ 30–35° stoop and a heavy cast-iron balustrade ending in a large newel.
2. Half-sunk basement behind a continuous iron areaway fence, with the basement door tucked under the stoop.
3. Window heights stepping down by storey, every opening with a projecting hood.
4. Dark, deep bracketed cornice in one continuous line along the row.
5. Uniform chocolate-brown smooth stone with almost invisible joints, darker iron and cornice against it.
6. A row of mirrored pairs, with doors and stoops coupled.
