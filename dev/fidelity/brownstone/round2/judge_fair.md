# Blind judgement: 16 elevations (round 2)

Judged by looking only at `images/img00.png`…`img15.png` and at photographs on Wikimedia Commons.
I judge massing, rhythm, proportion, hierarchy and characteristic elements. The flat colour rendering is ignored.

## What the set is

All 16 read at once as **New York rowhouses** (Brooklyn / Manhattan), the stoop-and-cornice type of **c. 1850–1875**:
three-bay façades, door in a side bay, a high stoop to a parlor floor over an English basement, an iron areaway fence,
and a bracketed metal cornice. Two families:

* **Brownstone-faced Italianate** (00, 02, 04, 06, 08, 09, 10, 12, 13, 15): about 1860–1875.
* **Brick with brownstone basement and trim** (01, 03, 07, 14): Brooklyn Greek Revival / early Italianate, about 1845–1860.
* Two outliers: 05 (round-arched, rusticated) and 11/12 (pilastered tripartite fronts) do not belong to this type.

The type is recognisable in every image. What gives the set away is the same small group of errors, repeated.

## Per drawing

R = recognisability (1–5), P = plausibility (1–5).

### img00: Brooklyn/Manhattan Italianate brownstone, c. 1865. Basement + 3. R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* The stoop is a blank slab with no treads. Every real stoop shows 12–16 tread nosings in elevation (A, B, D).
* Top-floor lintels touch the cornice bracket feet. In B there is a clear frieze of roughly 2–3 ft between the top-floor hoods and the cornice.
* A horizontal line is drawn at every floor, so the façade reads as stacked boxes. Real brownstone ashlar is continuous; the horizontals come from hoods, sills and one belt course at the parlor floor (A, B).
* The door hood is a soft segmental blob on thin pilasters, with no consoles. Compare the S-scroll consoles in C.
* Parlor / second-floor window height ratio is about 1.25. That is acceptable but weak; in A and B it is closer to 1.4–1.5.

### img01: Brooklyn brick rowhouse with brownstone basement, c. 1850. Basement + 3. R 3 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* The cornice is a thin flat board with tiny blocks. It is believable for very plain Greek Revival (H), but on a 3-storey house it is underscaled: about 2% of the height, where 5–8% is normal.
* The brownstone basement band starts at the parlor window sill on the right and not at the parlor floor. The stoop cheek blocks cut into it at a different height. The two levels should match.
* The stoop has no railings or newels at all, while the areaway has a fence. Every stoop had either iron railings or a stone balustrade.
* Same slab-stoop problem as 00.

### img02: Italianate brownstone, c. 1868. Basement + 3. R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* The door (round-arched transom under a triangular pediment) is the best in the set.
* The top-floor hoods are sliced off by the cornice: you see only the lower half of a hood. That is a clipping error no builder would make.
* The parlor windows have the same eared hood as the second floor. The parlor should be visibly richer, with a full enframement or a hood on consoles (B).
* There are no floor lines here, and it reads better for it.

### img03: Brick Italianate with brownstone basement, c. 1860. Basement + 3. R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* The pedimented round-arched door hood in a brick front is plausible (H, I).
* The stoop is a slab again. The newel and pole sit outside the stoop face at bottom left and stand on the facade edge.
* Top-floor lintels are fused into the cornice.
* There are floor lines on a brick wall. Brick fronts show no floor lines at all.

### img04: Two-storey-and-basement brownstone, c. 1860. R 3 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* The type exists (Park Slope and Carroll Gardens side streets), so the massing is fine.
* The stoop has no railing or newel. Its cheek walls step in plan (a wider block above, narrower below), and that does not read as stairs.
* The cornice is as deep as on the 4-storey houses, so it is heavy here: about 9% of the height.
* There is no areaway fence on the stoop side, and the stoop runs straight into the party-wall edge on the right.

### img05: Wide 2-storey + basement, round-arched, rusticated base. Reads as a hybrid. R 2 · P 2 · **NO**
* The banded rustication lines are **not level**: they visibly slope across the façade. That is the single most obvious "not drawn by an architect" signal in the set.
* The rustication runs straight through the window and door enframements instead of stopping against them.
* Round-arched heads sit inside square frames with a small keystone floating above the frame. A keystone belongs at the arch crown, in the arch.
* The proportion is about 1:1.3 (wide and squat) with a thin flat cornice. Round-arched rows in Brooklyn are the 1880s–90s Romanesque type (J), which has heavier, rock-faced detail and no Italianate stoop vocabulary. This is neither type.

### img06: Italianate brownstone, c. 1868. Basement + 3. R 3 · P 2 · **WITH FLAWS**
* On the top floor, two bays have segmental heads and the third is a plain rectangle. No row builder mixed window-head shapes within one floor.
* There is no areaway railing, only stoop newels.
* There is a doubled line just above the basement (two belt courses about 1 ft apart).
* The top-floor hoods are clipped by the cornice.

### img07: Brick with brownstone basement, door left, c. 1855. Basement + 3. R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* Good overall massing; it is the most "Cobble Hill" of the set (H).
* Small stepped stoop pieces stick out **past the left façade edge** at grade, so the stoop overruns the party line. Real stoops are contained within the lot width.
* The left newel straddles the facade edge.
* Floor lines are drawn on the brick wall.

### img08: Narrow 5-storey (basement + 4) brownstone, round-arched parlor, c. 1860. R 3 · P 2 · **WITH FLAWS**
* Very tall and narrow, about 1:2.6. That happens in Manhattan, but the top floor is not reduced enough to match.
* The parlor windows are round-arched under flat hoods (correct, see G), but they are only about 1.15× the second-floor height, so the parlor does not dominate.
* The top floor mixes heads again: two segmental, one flat.
* With five storeys of identical eared hoods, the façade reads as a stack with no hierarchy.

### img09: Italianate brownstone, c. 1868. Basement + 3. R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* One of the best in the set: consistent segmental top floor, eared hoods, pedimented round-arched door.
* The stoop's lower steps protrude **past the right façade edge** at grade.
* The top-floor hoods merge into the cornice.
* Floor lines.

### img10: Italianate brownstone, c. 1865. Basement + 3. R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* Same quality as 00.
* There is no areaway fence, so the basement windows face the sidewalk unprotected. Real houses had a railing or a low stone wall.
* The stoop steps poke out past the right façade edge.
* The window lintels are only flat bands on all floors, with no hood hierarchy.

### img11: Brownstone with a pilastered, tripartite parlor front. R 2 · P 2 · **NO**
* The parlor floor has a wide centre window flanked by narrow sidelights between pilaster strips. Those pilasters run down through the basement, but the upper floors keep the standard three bays. That reads as a commercial or 1900s remodel grafted on, not an original 1860s parlor.
* The pilaster rhythm is irregular (two different widths and spacings), and the strips cut through the sill and lintel bands.
* The stoop cheek walls widen at the bottom. That is right in plan, but in this elevation it reads as a step in the masonry.

### img12: Brownstone with pilastered tripartite upper floors. R 2 · P 1 · **NO**
* **The vertical axes do not line up.** The top floor has three evenly spaced windows, while the floors below have door bay + narrow + wide + narrow. The top-floor middle window lands over nothing. Bay axes are the one thing a rowhouse never breaks.
* The pilasters start at the second floor and stop under the top floor, so they do not organise the façade.
* The door bay is plain while everything else is pilastered. The hierarchy is inverted.

### img13: Italianate brownstone, round-arched parlor, c. 1865. Basement + 3. R 3 · P 2 · **WITH FLAWS**
* Round-arched parlor windows under flat hoods match G.
* The top floor mixes heads: the first bay is flat, the other two are segmental.
* The stoop has a notch halfway down (a wider block over a narrower one) and a sliver sticking out at the right edge at grade.
* The stoop has no railing poles, only the areaway fence.

### img14: Brick two-storey-and-basement, c. 1855. R 3 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* The massing is plausible (H).
* Stepped stoop pieces stick out **beyond the left façade edge**.
* The upper windows are as tall as the parlor windows (about 1.0×). On a two-storey house the parlor should still be about 1.3× taller.
* The window heads are jammed under the cornice brackets.

### img15: Wide brownstone, 2 storeys + basement, c. 1865. R 3 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* Consistent segmental upper floor with eared parlor hoods. The hierarchy is correct.
* It is wide for its height (about 1:1.2). That is plausible for a 25 ft lot, but the bays are spread so far that the piers are wider than the windows. Real piers are about 0.6–0.9× the window width.
* The slab stoop and the top-floor heads touching the cornice are the same issues as in the others.

## Ranked giveaways across all 16, with fixes

1. **The stoop is a blank slab (all 16).** It is the most characteristic element of the type and the least resolved.
   *Fix:* draw tread nosings at about 7 in risers (parlor-floor height ÷ 0.58 ft gives about 14–17 lines, slightly overhanging). Flank the stair with **stone cheek walls / balustrades 14–18 in wide**, sloping down to a **newel about 2 × 2 ft, 3.5–4 ft high** at the sidewalk (A, F). On brownstone fronts use a stone balustrade about 60% of the time and cast iron about 40%. On brick fronts use iron about 90% of the time (C, E, H).

2. **The stoop overruns the façade or party line (07, 09, 10, 13, 14, and the newel in 03).**
   *Fix:* clamp the stoop and its newels to inside the façade width, at least 4 in clear of the edge. The steps must never extend sideways past the lot line.

3. **Top-floor windows and hoods jammed into or clipped by the cornice (about 13 of 16).**
   *Fix:* keep a frieze band of **2–3 ft (0.6–0.9 m)** between the top of the top-floor hood and the underside of the cornice bracket feet (B). If the space isn't there, shorten the top-floor windows: they should be about 0.75–0.85× the second-floor height, not squeezed upward.

4. **A horizontal line at every floor (most drawings).** It turns a single ashlar face into stacked panels.
   *Fix:* delete the floor lines. Keep **one** belt or water-table course at the parlor floor (top of the basement). Optionally add a sill course under the parlor windows. Brick fronts get no floor lines at all.

5. **Weak parlor-floor hierarchy (02, 08, 10, 14, and to a degree most of the others).**
   *Fix:* make the parlor windows **1.35–1.5×** the second-floor height, with sills 1–1.5 ft above the floor. Floors then diminish, about 1.0 : 0.9 : 0.8 going up. Give the parlor the richest surround (full enframement, or a cornice on consoles, as in B). Upper floors get simpler hoods, and the top floor the simplest.

6. **Mixed window-head shapes within one floor (06, 08, 13).**
   *Fix:* pick the head shape per floor, never per bay. Combinations across floors such as an arched parlor with flat uppers are fine (G).

7. **Door enframement lacks consoles and projection (all).** In real brownstones the doorway is the most sculptural element.
   *Fix:* a hood or cornice about **1.6–1.9×** the door-opening width, carried on visible **S-scroll consoles** (C). Clear opening about 4.5–5 ft wide by 10–11 ft high, including the transom. Use a round-arched or segmental head about 70% of the time and a square transom about 30%. Turn the segmental "blob" into a proper segmental pediment with a moulded cornice.

8. **Broken bay axes and non-type pilastered fronts (11, 12).**
   *Fix:* every opening, from basement to top floor, must sit on one of the 3 (or 2) bay axes. Drop the tripartite and pilaster variants, or limit them to below 5% and require them to keep the three-bay axes on every floor.

9. **Sloping rustication and arch details (05).**
   *Fix:* rustication courses must be exactly level, 14–18 in high, and stop against the enframements. The keystone goes at the arch crown. Use full round-arched rows only with a Romanesque vocabulary, or drop them.

10. **Cornice reads as generic and evenly toothed.**
    *Fix:* put brackets on the piers: a pair at each end plus one over each pier between windows, with small modillions or dentils in between and raised panels in the frieze. Scale the cornice to **5–8% of façade height** (thinner in 01 and 05, heavier in 04). End it within 3–4 in of the façade edge rather than overhanging sideways, because these are party-wall houses.

11. **Missing areaway enclosure (06, 10, and the stoop side of 04).**
    *Fix:* always enclose the areaway with an iron fence about 3.5 ft high, or a low stone wall with railing, from the stoop to the far party line.

12. **Identical kit across the set.** The same fence, newel, four-panel door and lintel appear in every drawing, which gives the generator away when the drawings are seen together.
    *Fix:* vary at least the door (glazed upper panels, or 2 vs 4 panels), the fence finial and pattern, and the newel (iron vs stone). Also vary the sash: 2-over-2 vs 1-over-1, about 50/50.

**Best of the set:** 09, 02, 00, 07 (they would pass as real at a glance once items 1–4 are fixed).
**Worst:** 12, 05, 11.
