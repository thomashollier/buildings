# Havana round 2: sceptical blind judgement

Judge: independent architectural historian, blind. I looked only at `images/img00.png` to `img15.png` and at photographs on Wikimedia Commons. I judged massing, rhythm, proportion, hierarchy and characteristic elements, and ignored the flat colour rendering. Each drawing was assumed fake until it convinced me.

Scale: **R** = recognisability as Havana (1-5); **P** = plausibility as a real building (1-5). "Expert at a glance" means a specialist in Caribbean colonial and republican architecture looking for about five seconds.

## Two defects that appear in most drawings

These two account for most of the "NO" and "WITH FLAWS" verdicts below, so I state them once here.

* **D1, the floating pretil.** In img00, 02, 03, 04, 05, 10, 11, 12, 13 and 14 the parapet (pretil, balustrade or crest) is drawn as a separate band with a strip of **sky between it and the cornice**, about 0.3 to 0.5 m at the drawn scale. No Havana building has this. The pretil always sits on the top of the cornice and the cornice's top fillet carries straight into it (PRA right-hand house, MAL, GAL). A specialist notices it immediately, and a lay viewer often does too.
* **D2, identical openings on every floor.** In the grand fronts every storey repeats the same opening with the same surround, balcony, brackets and fanlight. Real Havana fronts grade from floor to floor. The principal floor gets the tallest openings, the richest surrounds and usually the continuous balcony. The floor above is lower (roughly 85 to 90 % of the principal floor's height), with plainer heads and separate balconies or just a balcony rail (PRA, MAL, GAL).

## Per-drawing verdicts

### img00
* **Reading:** Centro Habana or Prado, neoclassical, c. 1860-1890, three storeys, four bays.
* **Scores:** R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* **Giveaways:**
  * D1: the pretil and its central shaped crest float above the cornice. The crest also overlaps a pier rather than sitting over the central axis, because with four bays the centre is a pier.
  * Triangular pediments repeat on both upper floors. On the first floor they jam up against the brackets of the second-floor balconies, with the pediment apex touching the bracket tips. In Havana, pedimented heads are kept for the principal floor and the upper floor gets a flat cornice or a plain head (PRA).
  * The ground-floor windows have solid panelled leaves with **no reja**. A Havana street-level window without iron or wooden bars is very unusual before c. 1950 (TRI, JAR).
  * The zaguán door is shorter than the upper balcony doors. It should be the tallest opening on the front, usually 4.5 to 5.5 m.

### img01
* **Reading:** Habana Vieja modest two-storey colonial house, late 18th or early 19th century.
* **Scores:** R 3 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* **Giveaways:**
  * The upper openings are full-height louvred doors with **no balcony and no rail**. That is a door opening onto the void. Each one needs either a balcony or a sill at about 0.9 m.
  * The ground-floor reja is short (about 1.9 m), set high and flush with the wall. Colonial ventanas de reja run from about 0.4 to 0.6 m above the pavement almost up to the door head, project on a stone repisa and have a guardapolvo hood (TRI).
  * The coping is a flat red stripe. The colonial top is either a tejaroz (tile eave on wooden canes) or a moulded cornice with a pretil (JAR, TRI).
  * About 40 % of the ground-floor wall above the openings is blank, so the openings read as too short for a 5 to 6 m colonial ground storey.

### img02
* **Reading:** Centro Habana, eclectic or late neoclassical, c. 1900-1920, three storeys with shops.
* **Scores:** R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* **Giveaways:**
  * D1: a plain pretil and crest float above the cornice.
  * D2: both upper floors are identical, each with separate balconies on three brackets.
  * The corner pilasters are interrupted at each string course and have no capital at the cornice, so they read as stacked posts rather than an order.
  * The roller shutters are plausible for today, but they fill the ground storey up to the string course with no fascia or sign band, whereas GAL shows a lintel or sign band above the shop openings.
  * The dark zócalo stops at the shutters and the door, which is correct.

### img03
* **Reading:** Centro Habana or Cerro, neoclassical with mediopuntos, c. 1870-1890.
* **Scores:** R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* **Giveaways:**
  * D1: the balustered pretil floats.
  * The mediopunto is the same saturated radial red, yellow, green and blue fan on all eleven openings, including the small ground-floor windows. Real mediopuntos vary in pattern from floor to floor, and the colonial ones are mostly wooden louvre fans (JAR), with coloured glass mainly in the large ground and principal-floor arches.
  * The guardavecinos (fan-shaped iron spikes) sit at the ends of the continuous balcony, but they are drawn **inside the corner pilasters** rather than at the party wall line, where they actually stand.
  * The paired corner pilasters plus intermediate pilasters give five pilaster lines for four bays, which is acceptable. However, the flat hoods sit above the arch heads with a visible gap, so they look as if they are pinned on.

### img04
* **Reading:** Calzada de Reina, Monte or Galiano, narrow portal house, c. 1880-1910, two bays.
* **Scores:** R 4 · P 2 · **NO**
* **Giveaways:**
  * D1: the pretil and crest float.
  * With two bays, the axis falls on a **central pilaster**, which is uncomfortable but does occur. The real problem is the portal. Its square piers have caps but carry **no arch and no visible beam depth**: the drawing goes straight from the cap to the balcony slab. GAL and PRA show a deep lintel or entablature, about 0.6 to 0.9 m, over portal piers, and JAR and MAL show round arches.
  * The continuous balcony projects from the portal face, so it would hang over the street beyond the portal colonnade. That is possible, but here its brackets sit directly on the pier caps.
  * The upper pilasters (paired at the corners) do not land on the portal piers below.

### img05
* **Reading:** Centro Habana, neoclassical with mediopuntos, c. 1880-1900.
* **Scores:** R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* **Giveaways:**
  * D1: the pretil floats, and the pretil band here is a full 0.9 m tall with nothing on it.
  * D2: both upper floors are identical, with no continuous balcony on the principal floor. A Havana palacete with giant pilasters would normally have one.
  * The giant pilasters are white and stop at each string course, so they read as stacked pilasters rather than a giant order.
  * Only the door gets a mediopunto at ground level and the other three bays are roller shutters. A front this formal would have arched shop openings to match the upper arches.

### img06
* **Reading:** Cerro, Jesús María or Regla, one-storey vernacular house.
* **Scores:** R 2 · P 2 · **NO**
* **Giveaways:**
  * About 60 % of the front is blank wall. A one-storey colonial or 19th-century casa has a ground storey of 4.5 to 6 m with openings rising to within about 0.8 m of the cornice.
  * The reja is short and set mid-wall.

  * The door and window heads are at different heights; real fronts align them.

### img07
* **Reading:** Habana Vieja colonial house with portal and entresuelo, 18th century (Plaza Vieja or Plaza de San Francisco type).
* **Scores:** R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* **Giveaways:**
  * The **balcony brackets cut into the entresuelo window heads** directly below them, with the bracket tips touching the window frames.
  * The roof is a deep hip with no tejaroz or eave on canes. JAR shows that the colonial roof edge is a tiled alero over a moulded cornice.
  * The portal piers are thin square posts with no arch and no deep beam. Plaza Vieja portales are round Tuscan columns carrying semicircular arches (JAR) or a heavy wooden or stone lintel (PV).
  * The wooden balusters and the louvre fanlights are good and convincing.

### img08
* **Reading:** Habana Vieja colonial casa, two storeys, three bays, c. 1780-1820.
* **Scores:** R 4 · P 4 · **WITH FLAWS**
* **Giveaways:** This is the best of the set.
  * The hipped tile roof has no alero overhang. It needs about 0.4 to 0.6 m of tile eave on canes (TRI, JAR).
  * The iron balconies on stone slabs with three brackets are 19th-century details on an 18th-century body. They would pass, but a wooden balcony with turned balusters, or a continuous one, would be more convincing.
  * The upper-floor wall above the door heads (about 1.6 m) is a little high.
  * The ground-floor rejas, with repisa and guardapolvo, are correct.

### img09
* **Reading:** Habana Vieja or Cerro transitional neoclassical, c. 1830-1850.
* **Scores:** R 3 · P 2 · **NO**
* **Giveaways:**
  * Full-height white pilasters run **into the roof fascia with no entablature and no cornice**. Applying an order requires an entablature, and a Havana neoclassical house would have a cornice and a pretil, not an exposed tiled hip.
  * The upper doors are solid panels with no louvres or transom, which reads as closed and heavy for Havana.
  * The ground-floor windows are small relative to a roughly 5 m storey.

### img10
* **Reading:** Centro Habana narrow three-storey, c. 1890-1910.
* **Scores:** R 3 · P 2 · **NO**
* **Giveaways:**
  * The **hierarchy is inverted**: the continuous balcony is on the top floor and the separate balconies are on the principal floor. In Havana it is the other way round, with the continuous balcony on the principal floor (PRA, MAL).
  * D1: the pretil floats.
  * The two zaguán-type doors are identical, whereas one of them would normally be a shop opening.
  * The giant pilasters at the edges only, with nothing between, leave the front looking empty.

### img11
* **Reading:** Centro Habana eclectic, c. 1910-1925.

### img13
* **Reading:** Calzada de Monte or Reina portal building, c. 1880-1910, three storeys plus portal.
* **Scores:** R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* **Giveaways:**
  * The **balcony brackets bite into the portal arch crowns**. In GAL, MAL and PRA a portal carries a full entablature, about 0.8 to 1.2 m deep, between the arch crowns and the balcony slab.
  * The arches are faceted polygons that spring from simple square piers with thin caps. Real portal arches are true semicircles or segments, usually with archivolts and keystones (MAL, JAR).
  * D1: the balustrade floats.
  * D2: both upper floors are identical.

### img14
* **Reading:** Centro Habana (Galiano or Neptuno), five-storey eclectic, c. 1910-1930.
* **Scores:** R 4 · P 3 · **WITH FLAWS**
* **Giveaways:**
  * There is the same collision between the brackets and the arch crowns as in img13. The brackets here sit right on the arches.
  * D1: the pretil and crest float.
  * The three upper floors are identical, including the segmental doors, the keystones floating above the frames and the balconies. At five storeys a real building would vary the top floor, attic or crowning.
  * The thin corner pilasters run for five storeys with no base, belt or capital treatment.

### img15
* **Reading:** Habana Vieja 18th-century house with entresuelo and wooden balcony.
* **Scores:** R 3 · P 2 · **NO**
* **Giveaways:**
  * The wooden balcony has **posts (pies derechos) that rise to about 2.5 m and carry nothing**. Colonial wooden balconies have posts only because they support a tejaroz, a small tiled roof. Either add the tejaroz or remove the posts.
  * A string course separates the ground floor from the entresuelo, whereas planta baja and entresuelo read as one tall storey. The zaguán door should rise to about 4.5 m with the entresuelo windows above it.
  * About 55 % of the ground storey is blank wall.
  * The balcony slab is a flat grey board, not timber joists or a moulded stone.

## Ranking of giveaways across all 16, with fixes

1. **Floating pretil (D1), 10 of 16 drawings.** Set the gap between the cornice top and the pretil base to **0**. The pretil base should be the top fillet of the cornice. Keep the pretil about 0.9 to 1.2 m tall, and centre any crest on the central axis, which with an even number of bays means the central pier, so give the crest the width of the two middle bays.
2. **Brackets colliding with what is below, in img00, 07, 13 and 14.** Before placing a balcony, check that the bottom of the bracket (slab underside minus about 0.4 m) clears the head of the opening or the arch crown below by **at least 0.3 m**. Over a portal, insert an entablature or beam of **0.8 to 1.2 m** between the arch crown and the slab. Otherwise use shallower brackets (0.25 m), or drop them in favour of a moulded slab.
3. **No hierarchy between floors (D2), in img00, 02, 05, 13 and 14; inverted in img10.** Make the principal floor (the first floor above ground) **about 10 to 15 % taller** than the floor above. Give it the continuous balcony with **odds of about 0.6** on grand fronts (and **never** put the continuous balcony only on the top floor). Use pediments or richer heads only on the principal floor and simple cornice heads above. At four storeys or more, make the top floor lower again, at about 85 %.
4. **Short openings and blank walls on ground floors, in img01, 06, 12 and 15.** The ground-storey door head should be at **0.78 to 0.85 × the storey height**, and ventanas de reja should run from **0.4 to 0.6 m** above the pavement to the door-head line. Rejas should project **0.15 to 0.3 m** on a repisa and have a guardapolvo.
5. **Openings with no protection, in img00 (ground floor) and img01 (upper floor).** Every street-level window should have a reja (wooden barrotes before c. 1850, iron after). Every upper door-height opening should have a balcony or a balconette rail, with probability 1.
6. **Monotonous mediopuntos, in img03, 05, 11 and 12.** Draw the pattern from a set and **vary it between floors**: wooden louvre fan or *abanico* (JAR) with odds of about 0.5 on colonial fronts, and radial or concentric coloured glass on 19th-century ones. Never use a square grid cut by the arc. Small ground-floor windows should usually have a plain head rather than a mediopunto.
7. **Roof edges, in img06, 07, 08, 09 and 15.** A visible tiled hip needs a **tejaroz or alero of 0.4 to 0.6 m on canes** and no applied order. A flat red stripe is not a known Havana element: use either a moulded cornice plus pretil or a tile eave. If the front has pilasters, it must have an entablature and cornice and a flat roof behind a pretil (img09).
8. **Portal supports, in img04, 07, 13 and 14.** Use round Tuscan columns with semicircular arches for colonial work (JAR), and square piers with a deep lintel or round arches with archivolts for 19th- and 20th-century work (GAL, PRA, MAL). Never use faceted polygonal arches. Align the upper pilasters with the portal supports below.
9. **Balcony posts that carry nothing, in img15.** Pies derechos should appear only together with a tejaroz over the balcony.
10. **Guardavecinos placed inside the corner pilasters, in img03, 12 and 14.** Put them at the party-wall line, at the very end of the balcony slab.

* **Highest-value fix:** fixing D1 alone would lift five of the drawings by about one point of P.
